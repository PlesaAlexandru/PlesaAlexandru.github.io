## Scenario:

As a member of the IT department at SwiftSpend Financial, you are responsible for assisting employees with technical concerns. What initially appeared to be a routine day quickly escalated when multiple employees across different departments reported receiving a suspicious email. Several users noted unusual characteristics in the message, and unfortunately, some had already submitted their credentials and were no longer able to access their accounts. With the potential for a wider compromise, the incident has been escalated for investigation. Your task is to analyze the available evidence, determine the scope of the attack, and uncover how the adversary operated.

---
## Objective

- Analyze the provided email samples to identify key artifacts
- Investigate phishing URLs to understand redirection
- Retrieve and examine the phishing kit used in the attack
- Use CTI tools to gather intelligence on the adversary
- Analyze the phishing kit to uncover additional indicators

## Mail 1: derick.marshall

### Summary

- **Description:** Fake "Direct Credit Advice" with an HTML attachment that redirects to a fake Microsoft 365 password page. SPF, DKIM and DMARC do not align with the visible sender.
- **Receiver:** `derick[.]marshall[at]swiftspend[.]finance`
- **Sender:** Group Marketing Online Accounts Payable (`Accounts[.]Payable[at]groupmarketingonline[.]icu`)
- **Return-Path:** `9ZTKYQdyZIim[at]BRAEMARHOWELLS[.]COM`
- **Timestamp:** `28-06-2020 17:01:33 -0500` (22:01:33 UTC)
- **IOCs:**
  - `groupmarketingonline[.]icu`
  - `BRAEMARHOWELLS[.]COM`
  - `hxxp://kennaroads[.]buzz/data/Update365/office365/40e7baa2f826a57fcf04e5202526f8bd/enterpassword.php`
- **Attachment details:** `Direct Credit Advice.html`, 520 bytes, SHA-256 `c6ed70c3970a9ba62ef5f54be8608913a631f53e08e4914a4036299bb5d3ddb6`, VirusTotal 0/62

### The email as the user sees it

![](../../assets/Pasted%20image%2020261027134035.png)

![](../../assets/Pasted%20image%2020261027134102.png)

What stands out at first sight:

- The subject contains the recipient's own address (`... - derick.marshall@swiftspend.finance`). That is a mail-merge template, not something a real accounts payable team would write.
- Generic greeting (`Good Day!`) and no reference to any real transaction, invoice number or amount.
- The only content is an attachment, and the attachment is an `.html` file. A legitimate remittance advice would be a PDF or live in a portal.
- The signature looks polished but is empty on inspection: the phone numbers are `(800) 555-xxxx` placeholders and the address is nonsense (`Public Luggage 99929, Oldmarket, Yorkshire 1024, New Island`).
- The red `EXTERNAL SENDER` banner was added by the mail gateway. It did its job, but it only helps if the user reads it.

Authentication results, searched in the message source for `SPF`, `DKIM`, `DMARC` and `Return`:

What `log.txt` contains, with the passwords intentionally left out of this writeup:

- Entries for `michael.ascot`, `zoe.duncan`, `derick.marshall` and `michelle.chen` at `swiftspend.finance`. **That confirms credential theft for at least four accounts.**
- `michael.ascot` appears twice. That is consistent with the `retry.php` behaviour described below, where the victim is asked to enter the password a second time.
- The employee entries I could read in full come from the same client IP (`64.62.197.80`, United States) with the same user agent (Chrome 60 on Windows 10), all stamped `10:01 am`. Four different employees sharing one IP and browser in the same minute is not how real users behave. In a production case I would treat that as automation or a shared egress and not as four independent victims.

### Kit code: where the credentials go

![](../../assets/Pasted%20image%2020261027144500.png)

`submit.php` shows the exfiltration logic:

1. If the password is shorter than 6 characters, the user is sent back to `index.php?...&email=...&error=2` and has to type it again.
2. Otherwise, `mail()` sends the data to `m3npat[at]yandex[.]com`.
3. The victim is then redirected to `retry.php`, which asks again. This gives the attacker a second attempt and makes typos less likely to slip through.

<Info>The `&email=...&error=` pattern in the phishing URL from Mail 1 is the same pattern the kit uses in its redirects. That ties the URL we saw in the email to this exact kit.</Info>

## Recommended Actions (L1 handoff to L2 / IR)

**Containment**

1. Reset the passwords of the four confirmed accounts and revoke their active sessions and refresh tokens. Enforce MFA if it is not already required.
2. Block `kennaroads[.]buzz`, `groupmarketingonline[.]icu` and the sender and Return-Path addresses at the mail gateway, proxy and DNS.
3. Search all mailboxes for the sender, the Return-Path domains and the subject patterns (`Direct Credit Advice`, `Quote for Services Rendered`) and purge the messages that are still unread.

**Scoping**

4. Review Entra ID / Microsoft 365 sign-in logs for the five accounts, looking for logins from `64.62.197.80` or any new location or device after `28-06-2020 22:01 UTC`.
5. Check the affected mailboxes for new inbox rules, forwarding and OAuth app consents, which are the usual follow-up after a credential theft.
6. Search proxy and DNS logs for any other host that contacted `kennaroads[.]buzz`, since users who clicked but did not submit credentials still matter.
7. Check whether `william.mcclean` opened the PDF and clicked the link.

**Reporting and prevention**

8. Report the domain and the exposed kit to the registrar (Dynadot) and the hosting provider, and submit the samples to VirusTotal and URLhaus-style feeds.
9. Block external `.html` attachments, or detonate them in a sandbox before delivery.
10. Publish `p=reject` DMARC with SPF and DKIM for the company's own domain, so SwiftSpend cannot be spoofed the same way.
11. Run a short awareness reminder: the `EXTERNAL SENDER` banner was present on every mail and was still not enough.

---
## What I Would Improve in the Detection

- The gateway let through a message with `dkim=none`, `dmarc=none` and an SPF fail on one hop. A rule that raises the score of that exact combination for first-time external senders would have caught Mails 1 to 4.
- Mail 5 is the lesson that authentication results alone are not a verdict. A detection on `From` domain different from the DKIM `d=` and Return-Path domain, combined with an attachment containing a single link, would cover it.
- An HTML attachment of around 500 bytes is a strong signal on its own. Attachment size plus type is cheap to alert on.

---
## Conclusion

A well-organized credential harvesting campaign: five emails, one vendor identity, one landing server and two delivery tricks. The HTML redirectors have a clean VirusTotal score because the real signal is in the infrastructure, and the PDF mail passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC because it travels through a legitimate provider. In both cases the way to catch it is the same: do not stop at the verdict of a single check, pivot on the infrastructure and correlate the mails.

The exposed open directory made the investigation easy, since it gave us the kit, the exfiltration address and the harvested accounts. The result is a clear True Positive with four confirmed compromised accounts and a list of follow-up checks for L2.
